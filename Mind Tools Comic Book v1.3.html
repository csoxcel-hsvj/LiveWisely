For teachers and parents

How to use this book with children. You don’t need to be an expert in these ideas. The comics do the teaching; your job is to ask good questions and let children think out loud.

Run a 25-minute session

  1. 2 minStart with a problem

    Pick a card from “Got a problem? Start here” that fits your class or child. Ask: has this ever happened to you?

  2. 5 minRead the comic aloud

    Children read Meera, Kabir and GrandMa’s lines. Stop before panel 3 and ask what they would do.

  3. 8 minAnalogy and true story

    Read the story together. Ask: what did the people in the story miss? Point out the fact-check label.

  4. 8 minTry this

    Do one “Try this” activity now, or set it as a small task for the week.

  5. 2 minExit question

    Each child names one place in their own life where the tool’s question could help.

Pitch it to the age

Ages 10 to 12One tool a week. Lean on the comic, the analogy and the yellow takeaway box. Keep activities hands-on: dice, coins, drawing, role-play. Use the question cards as a game: you describe a situation, they pick the question.
Ages 13 to 17Two related tools per session, such as Social Proof with Confirmation Bias. Use the blue “going deeper” box. Apply tools to news, ads, social media and their own decisions. Ask them to argue against a tool: when could it mislead you?

Where to start

These six are the easiest to grasp and the most useful day to day. Begin here, then explore one family at a time.

In the classroom

  • Game Zone in teams: split the class into two teams and play one game on a projector. Pause after each round and ask: which thinking tool was hiding in that choice?
  • Tool of the week: put the week’s question on the board, and award a point whenever a student spots it in a lesson, story or the news.
  • Make your own comic: students draw three panels with Meera and Kabir facing a new problem that a tool solves.
  • Thinking journal: once a week, students write one decision they made and which question they used.
  • Across subjects: Survivorship Bias in history, Probabilistic Thinking in maths, Feedback Loops in science, Second-Order Thinking in civics.

At home

  • Family game night: play a Game Zone game in two teams, children against parents. Children usually win Mango Grove.
  • Dinner-table question: one question card per day. Everyone shares a time it fit their day.
  • Family decisions: planning a trip or a party? Run Inversion together: how could this go wrong?
  • Screen time: use Opportunity Cost and Feedback Loops to talk about it without lecturing.
  • Model it yourself: say your thinking out loud, like “I almost bought this because everyone has one. That’s social proof, so let me wait a week.”

Do

  • Praise good thinking, even when the answer is wrong.
  • Let children disagree with the tools and with you.
  • Ask for their own example; that is how you know it has clicked.
  • Admit when you fell into a trap yourself. Children copy that honesty.

Don’t

  • Don’t use the tools to win arguments with children (“That’s sunk cost, so you’re wrong”).
  • Don’t test definitions. Ask them to apply the question to a situation instead.
  • Don’t rush through all 23. Depth beats coverage.
  • Don’t treat any tool as a rule. Each is one lens, and good thinkers switch lenses.

Discussion prompts by family

A note on sensitive stories

A few stories touch on war, nuclear weapons or famine: Survivorship Bias, Hanlon’s Razor and Chesterton’s Fence. They are told gently, but preview them for younger or sensitive children, and leave room for questions.

Signs it’s working

  • Children use a question without being prompted (“wait, and then what?”).
  • They explain a tool with their own example.
  • They notice when a tool doesn’t fit, or when someone misuses one.
  • They check facts before believing a story, which is itself a thinking tool.

Sources and fact checking

The tool list draws on Farnam Street’s mental models library and Gabriel Weinberg’s “Mental Models I Find Repeatedly Useful” (the basis of his book Super Thinking, co-written with Lauren McCann). James Clear’s mental models guide and The Latticework (ltcwrk.com) are good further reading. Historical stories come from well-known historical records; where a story is a legend or its details are disputed, the chapter says so.
